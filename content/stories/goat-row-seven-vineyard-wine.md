Every vineyard has a pest. Most have birds. Some have deer. Quill Hollow Vineyards, forty acres of hillside in southern Oregon, has Hazel.

Hazel is a seven-year-old Nubian goat with ears like two oven mitts and a talent for latches. She was bought to clear blackberry brambles off the back slope. She cleared them in a month, and then she discovered the grapes.

What made Hazel strange was not that she ate grapes. Goats eat everything. What made Hazel strange was that she ate only one row.

"Forty rows on that slope," said owner and winemaker Delphine Okonkwo. "Same variety, same age, same irrigation. She walked past thirty-nine of them every single night to get to row seven. She'd eat the lower clusters, stand on her back legs for the higher ones, and walk home."

## The fence war

Delphine tried everything. She raised the fence. Hazel jumped it. She put a lock on the gate. Hazel learned to lift the gate off its hinges. She tried a motion light. Hazel, by every account, enjoyed it.

For two harvests, row seven came in at less than half of what the other rows produced. The vineyard's crew started calling it the Goat Tax.

> "I genuinely considered selling her. My husband said, 'Delphine, she's telling you something.' I said she was telling me she's a goat." — Delphine Okonkwo

## What the goat knew

In the third summer a soil consultant came out to look at a drainage problem on a different slope. Delphine mentioned the goat as a joke over lunch. The consultant asked to see row seven.

She took samples from the row and from the rows on either side. The difference was startling: row seven sits on a narrow seam of old volcanic rock that runs under the hill at an angle, just one vine-width wide. The vines there struggle harder for water, grow smaller berries, and concentrate more sugar and flavor in each one.

"She wasn't being random," the consultant told Delphine. "She was being picky."

## Bottling the goat's choice

That fall Delphine did something she had never done before. She picked row seven by itself, the clusters Hazel had left, and fermented them in a single small barrel. It made exactly ninety-four bottles.

She named the wine *Row Seven* and put a line drawing of a long-eared goat on the label.

In August, at a regional competition with more than six hundred entries, Row Seven won the vineyard's first gold medal. One judge wrote on the scoring sheet: "Unusual depth. Whoever chose this fruit knew exactly what they were doing."

Delphine has that sheet framed in the tasting room, next to a photograph of Hazel standing on her hind legs with a grape cluster in her mouth.

## A new arrangement

Hazel now has her own fenced paddock and a share of row seven by agreement: the bottom clusters on the first ten vines are hers, and the crew does not pick them. In return, every spring Delphine walks Hazel through the newly planted block on the north slope and writes down which rows she stops at.

"I know how it sounds," Delphine said. "But she's two for two."

The ninety-four bottles of the first vintage sold out in a weekend. The waiting list for next year's is longer than the row.
