Juniper has never cared much about tennis balls. She is a four-year-old golden retriever who prefers sticks: long ones, crooked ones, ones too big to fit through the door. So when she started bringing home something small, black and lumpy from the field behind the Lindqvist house in rural Kansas, the family assumed it was a strange-looking piece of wood.

It was not wood.

"I picked it up to throw it and nearly dropped it on my foot," said Nils Lindqvist, a high school wrestling coach. "It was the size of a baseball and heavy as a dumbbell. And kind of melted-looking on one side. I thought it was a piece of old farm machinery."

Juniper thought it was the best thing she had ever found. She carried it everywhere. She slept with her chin on it. When the kids hid it in the garage, she sat in front of the garage door and sighed until they gave it back.

## The neighbor with the magnet

It was the family's neighbor, a retired surveyor named Ottilie Brandvold, who suggested they test it. She held a refrigerator magnet near the rock. It jumped to the surface and stuck.

"Iron," she said. "A lot of it. Take that to the university."

Nils drove it two hours to a state university geology department in a cooler, with Juniper whining in the back seat the entire way.

## "We get forty of these a year. This one was real."

The geologist who examined it said the department receives dozens of suspected meteorites every year from hopeful finders, and almost all of them turn out to be slag, iron ore or ordinary dark rock.

This one had a thin, glossy fusion crust from burning through the atmosphere, small thumbprint-shaped dents called regmaglypts, and, when a tiny corner was polished and etched with acid, the crisscross metal pattern that only forms when iron cools slowly in space over millions of years.

It was an iron meteorite, roughly 1.9 pounds. A collector's appraisal put its value at about $41,000.

> "I asked if Juniper could come in for the photo. The geologist said, 'Absolutely, she found it.'" — Nils Lindqvist

## What the money became

The family talked about it for a week. The kids wanted a pool. Nils wanted to fix the roof. His wife, Annika, a veterinary nurse, pointed out that the town of 1,900 people had no place where dogs could run off-leash, and that most of their neighbors' dogs spent their days on chains or behind small fences.

In the end they sold the meteorite to a natural history museum, which agreed to display it with a photograph of the dog who found it. They fixed the roof. With the rest, they bought a two-acre lot at the edge of town and fenced it.

The Juniper Dog Run opened in September. It has a water station, two benches, a pile of logs for climbing, and a hand-painted sign at the gate that reads: *Found by a dog. Built for dogs.*

On opening day more than sixty dogs came. Juniper ignored all of them, walked straight to the far corner of the field and started digging.

"We're letting her," Annika said. "Obviously."
