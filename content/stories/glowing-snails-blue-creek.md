In the hollow below Shiloh Run Road, the old-timers have a name for it: the ghost light. For three nights every September, usually right after the first cool rain, the creek glows. Not brightly. Not everywhere. But stand on the footbridge after dark and the stones in the shallows shimmer with a faint, cold blue, like someone scattered a handful of stars into the water.

Nobody in the hollow ever knew what it was. Fox fire, some said: the glowing fungus that grows on rotting logs. Swamp gas, said others. One man swore for forty years it was the reflection of a radio tower that, it was later pointed out, does not exist.

Wren Oduya, who is twelve, wanted a real answer.

## The jar

Wren lives at the top of the hollow with her grandmother, who first took her down to the footbridge to see the ghost light when she was five. Every September since, they have gone back.

This year she brought a mason jar, a flashlight with a red filter ("so I don't scare anything") and a notebook.

"I waded out to where it was brightest and turned my light on," she said. "And all the glowing stopped. And there were just a lot of snails."

Small ones, each about the size of a pea, with thin, almost see-through shells. She scooped three of them into her jar with some creek water, turned off her flashlight and held the jar up.

They glowed.

## "I had to sit down on the rock"

Her grandmother, Mavis Oduya, was on the footbridge.

"She came up the bank holding that jar out in front of her like it was the Olympic torch," Mavis said. "Three little blue lights in a jelly jar. I'd been looking at that creek for sixty-one years. I had to sit down on the rock."

Wren's science teacher helped her contact a biology professor at the state university. The professor came out the next night, waded into the creek with Wren, and spent two hours counting.

> "There are glowing snails in the ocean. There are a few known on land in other parts of the world. As far as anyone could tell, nobody had written down a freshwater one here. That's not nothing." — the professor, who asked to be identified only as Dr. A.

## Why only three nights?

Over the next weeks, Wren and the professor worked out a theory. The snails seem to live hidden under the creek stones most of the year. After the first cool September rain they come out to mate, and the glow, they think, is how they find each other in the dark.

Three nights. Then they're gone again until next year.

Samples have been sent to a lab to find out exactly what the snail is. If it turns out to be new, the professor told Wren, she will get to suggest a name.

She already has one picked out. She won't say what it is.

"It's a surprise," she said. "But my grandma's going to cry."
