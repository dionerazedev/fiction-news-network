The first thing Marisol Teague noticed was the sea turtles.

Marisol manages the gift shop at the Tidewater Marine Center, a small aquarium on the Maine coast. Every evening she lines the plush toys up on the back wall by species: whales on the top shelf, seals in the middle, sea turtles at the bottom.

Every morning, the sea turtles were on the front table.

"Not all of them," she said. "Six or seven. Always the green ones. Lined up in a neat row facing the door, like they were waiting to greet customers."

## The suspects

Marisol blamed the night cleaners. The cleaners said they never went in the gift shop. She changed the locks. The turtles kept moving. A volunteer suggested a ghost, and for one week the staff break room took the idea surprisingly seriously.

Then things got stranger. A rack of postcards was turned to face the wall. A basket of rubber crabs was emptied, the crabs sorted by color, and the basket left upside down. Someone, or something, had left a wet trail across the counter.

That trail was the clue. It led out the back of the shop, along a service corridor and up a short ladder to the top of the open-water tank, where the aquarium's giant Pacific octopus lives.

## Pudding

Pudding is about four years old, weighs roughly forty pounds and has an arm span longer than a pool table. Her keepers describe her as "curious, picky about shrimp, and extremely good at lids."

The aquarium put a camera in the gift shop. On the second night it recorded a reddish shape sliding under the door shortly after two in the morning. For forty minutes, Pudding explored. She opened drawers. She tasted a keychain. She lifted a plush sea turtle off the bottom shelf, held it in front of her eye for a long time, and set it gently on the front table.

Then she did it again. And again. Then she went home and closed the lid of her tank behind her.

> "We watched that footage maybe thirty times. There's a moment where she adjusts one turtle a tiny bit so it lines up with the others. That's when I started crying." — Marisol Teague

## The turtle effect

The aquarium fixed the gap at the top of the tank, which is now secured with a lid Pudding has not yet beaten. But Marisol did something else, too: she left the green sea turtles on the front table.

Sales of the plush turtle went up more than 300 percent in the next two months. The shop now sells a small card with each one: *Chosen by Pudding.*

Staff say there is no great mystery to why an octopus might single out one toy. Octopuses are intensely curious and drawn to particular colors and textures. The green plush has bumpy, scale-like stitching, a little like the shells of the crabs she eats.

Marisol prefers another theory.

"She has taste," she said. "Honestly, better than mine. I'd been hiding the best thing in the shop on the bottom shelf for years."

Pudding now gets a supervised "shopping trip" every Friday morning before the aquarium opens, carried to a small tray of toys in a bucket of seawater. Whatever she picks goes on the front table for the week. Last week it was a rubber lobster. It sold out by Sunday.
