The beaver dam on Loon Bottom Creek is enormous: nearly ninety feet across, waist-high in places, and old enough that small birch trees grow out of its top. Generations of beavers have patched it, raised it and argued over it with the county road crew.

What nobody knew until this summer was that the whole thing was built around a canoe.

Retired schoolteachers Hollis and Bea Marchetti spotted it in August, after a dry spell dropped the creek nearly two feet. Sticking out of the base of the dam was the curved bow of a wooden boat, its green paint faded almost to grey.

"I said, 'Hollis, that's a canoe,'" Bea said. "He said, 'That's a log.' Forty-one years of marriage and he still says that."

It was a canoe.

## Built in, stick by stick

The Marchettis came back with a state wildlife officer, who was careful not to disturb the active dam. Working from the downstream side, the officer could see how the beavers had used the boat like a beam: sticks woven over and around the hull, mud packed into the gaps, the whole thing locked into the structure of the dam.

Wildlife biologists say beavers commonly build around fallen trees, fence posts and old farm equipment. "They're practical builders," the officer said. "A canoe wedged in the creek is a gift. Something long and solid to build on."

Under the stern seat, where the canoe had been tipped against the dam, the officer found a small glass jar with a metal lid sealed in wax. Inside was an envelope.

## The letter

The envelope was addressed in pencil to *Miss Clara Wendt, Pine Street, Ashby Falls* and dated July 1937. It had never been posted.

The Marchettis did not open it. Bea taught fourth grade for thirty years and has firm feelings about other people's mail.

"Somebody meant for Clara to read that," she said. "So we were going to find Clara."

> "We joked that we'd be the slowest mail carriers in history. Eighty-nine years late." — Hollis Marchetti

It took six weeks. Clara Wendt had married and become Clara Rasmussen in 1939. She had died in 2002. But the Ashby Falls historical society had a record of her family, and the trail led to her daughter, Ruth Rasmussen Kowalczyk, who is 84 and lives three counties away.

## "He meant to give it to her"

In September the Marchettis drove the jar to Ruth's house. She opened the envelope at her kitchen table with both of them sitting beside her.

The letter was from a young man named Arthur Pell. In careful, nervous handwriting, he told Clara he planned to paddle to her side of the lake on Saturday evening and that if she met him on the dock he would ask her something important.

Ruth sat with the page for a long time. Then she laughed.

"My father's name was Arthur Pell Rasmussen," she said. "My mother named him after a boy she knew who drowned on the lake the summer before she met Dad. She told me about him once. She said he was the first boy who ever made her laugh, and that he never got to say whatever he'd been meaning to say."

She paused.

"Well," she said. "Now he has."

Ruth has had the letter framed. The canoe is still in the dam. The county has agreed to leave it there.
