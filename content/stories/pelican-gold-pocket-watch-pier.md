Ezra Lightfoot has fished off the end of the Marigold Point pier on the Gulf Coast every morning for nineteen years. He knows the regulars: the retired nurse who catches nothing and doesn't mind, the brothers who argue about bait, and a big, battered brown pelican with a notch in its beak that everybody calls Admiral.

Admiral begs. Admiral steals. Admiral once took an entire sandwich out of a tourist's hand and swallowed it, wrapper and all.

So Ezra was not expecting a gift.

"He landed right next to my bucket, which he does about four times a morning," Ezra said. "And he made this coughing, gagging noise like he was about to bring up a fish. And instead, plink. Something gold on the boards."

It was a pocket watch: heavy, scratched, its chain tangled with seaweed and fishing line. The glass was cracked. The hands were stopped at 4:10.

## The engraving

Ezra took the watch home, soaked it in fresh water and pried open the back with a butter knife. Inside the case, in delicate script, was an engraving:

*To Abraham, from Lillian. For every hour we waited. June 1946.*

"I'm not a sentimental guy," Ezra said. "But that one got me."

A jeweler in town confirmed the watch was solid gold, made in the 1930s, and worth several thousand dollars, even damaged. Ezra could have sold it that afternoon.

Instead he posted a photograph of the engraving on the town's community page, with a question: *Does anyone know Abraham and Lillian?*

## Fifty years of looking

Within two days, a woman named Rosalind Hale called from Atlanta.

Abraham and Lillian were her grandparents. Abraham had come home from the war in 1946; Lillian, who had waited for him, gave him the watch on their wedding day. In 1976, on a fishing trip at Marigold Point, Abraham leaned over the rail to unhook a fish and the watch slid out of his shirt pocket into the water.

"He searched for it every summer for the rest of his life," Rosalind said. "He'd rent a little boat and drag a magnet off the end of the pier. My father kept going after Grandpa died. Then I did, a couple of times, when the kids were small. We all just thought it was gone."

> "Fifty years, three generations of us with magnets, and a pelican finds it." — Rosalind Hale

## Admiral's habit

How did a pelican end up with a watch that had been lost for half a century? The best guess from local wildlife rescuers is that a recent storm churned up the sand around the pilings, and Admiral, who scoops up anything shiny near the surface, grabbed it along with a mouthful of small fish and couldn't swallow it.

Pier regulars now say Admiral has a habit. In the weeks since, he has also delivered a fishing lure, a bottle cap and, on one memorable morning, a set of car keys belonging to the retired nurse.

## Home

Rosalind drove down to Marigold Point in September with her teenage son. Ezra met them at the end of the pier and handed her the watch. She held it for a long time, then opened the back and read the engraving out loud.

The family offered Ezra a reward. He asked them to give it to the wildlife rescue center that patches up the pier's pelicans instead.

The watch has been repaired. It runs. Rosalind wears it on a chain around her neck. And she has set it, she says, to stop at 4:10 every day, for a minute, on purpose.

Admiral has not been thanked formally. He has, according to Ezra, received an unusual number of free fish.
