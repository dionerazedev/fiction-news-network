Rudy Ballantyne has driven the night plow on Route 9 through the Tamarack Pass for sixteen winters. He knows every curve, every drift trap, every spot where the wind piles snow across the road like a sleeping dog. He has seen moose, owls, a lost llama and, once, a man in a full tuxedo walking uphill at three in the morning who declined a ride.

He had never seen anything like the fox at mile fourteen.

"First night, it's sitting right at the guardrail," Rudy said. "Not running across. Not hunting. Sitting. Staring at the truck like it was waiting for a bus."

The second night it was there again. The third night too, in the same spot, at the same hour, ears forward, with fresh snow on its back as if it had been sitting for a long time.

## "Like it was checking I was paying attention"

On the fourth night, Rudy slowed the plow to a crawl. The fox stood up, trotted twenty feet along the guardrail, stopped, and looked back over its shoulder.

"It did that three times," he said. "Walk, stop, look back. Like it was checking I was paying attention. My partner on the radio said, 'Rudy, you are not getting out of that truck for a fox.' So I didn't."

He thought about it all the next day.

On the fifth night, with the snow coming sideways and the radio quiet, Rudy pulled the plow into the turnout at mile fourteen, put on his headlamp, and climbed over the guardrail.

## The cabin below the road

The fox led him down a steep bank, through a stand of spruce and across a frozen creek. About a quarter mile from the road, in a clearing Rudy had never known existed, there was a small log cabin with no lights on and a roof bowed under four feet of snow.

Smoke was not coming from the chimney. A woman's voice was coming from inside.

Her name was Agatha Steen. She was 81, a retired botany teacher who had lived alone in the cabin every winter for thirty years. A week earlier she had slipped on her porch steps and broken her ankle. Her phone had died two days later. She had been rationing crackers and melting snow on a camp stove, and the fuel had run out that afternoon.

> "I said to the fox, every night when it came to the window, 'Go get somebody.' I talked to it like a dog. I never for one second thought it understood me." — Agatha Steen

Rudy radioed for help, wrapped her in his coat, and kept her talking for the ninety minutes it took a rescue crew to reach the clearing on snowmobiles. She was treated for dehydration and the broken ankle and was home in a month.

## The fox's side of the story

It turns out Agatha had been feeding the fox for years. Scraps of cheese, mostly, set out on the windowsill each evening. When she stopped coming to the window, the fox kept coming. When nobody answered, it went up to the road.

Wildlife experts the Paw Post asked were careful to say no one can know what was going on in the animal's head. "Foxes are very routine-driven," one said. "A broken routine can send them looking."

Rudy has a simpler explanation. "It was asking for help," he said. "I'm just glad I eventually got the message."

## Mile fourteen

Agatha spent the rest of that winter with her niece in town. In the spring she moved back to the cabin, now with a satellite phone, a new roof and a standing arrangement with the county: the night plow calls her at nine o'clock every evening on its way through the pass.

The fox still comes to her window. She still sets out cheese.

And every night at mile fourteen, Rudy flashes his amber lights twice as he passes the turnout. Most nights, he says, he sees a pair of eyes by the guardrail. He likes to think the fox knows who it is.
