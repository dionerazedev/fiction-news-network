Taco does not dig. That is the first thing everyone in Sagebrush Flats, Wyoming, tells you about Taco. The nine-year-old border collie herds cattle on the Delacroix ranch, rides in the truck with his head out the window, and sleeps on a feed sack on the porch. He has never shown the slightest interest in holes.

So when he spent an entire Saturday in May digging furiously beside the foundation of the old one-room schoolhouse at the edge of town, his owner, rancher Callum Delacroix, came over to see what was wrong.

At the bottom of a two-foot hole was the corner of a metal box.

## The box

It was an old steel ammunition box, rusted brown and sealed with tape that crumbled at a touch. Stenciled on the lid in white paint, still faintly readable, were the words *SAGEBRUSH FLATS SCHOOL — DO NOT OPEN UNTIL 2026.*

"It was May of 2026," Callum said. "So I figured we were allowed."

He did not open it himself. He drove it to the town hall, where the clerk called the mayor, who called the retired schoolteacher, who called everybody else.

## Fifty kids, one letter

Inside, wrapped in plastic bread bags, were drawings, a cassette tape, a 1976 bicentennial quarter, a class photograph of fifty children squinting in the sun and a single sheet of notebook paper signed by all of them.

The letter, written in the round, careful handwriting of a ten-year-old, read in part:

*Dear Future Sagebrush Flats. We are the fifth grade. We are burying this box because our school is closing and we have to go to the big school in Cody on the bus. We don't want anybody to forget this school. When you find this, please do one thing. Please have a picnic at the school and invite all of us back.*

At the bottom: *P.S. Whoever finds this gets to keep the quarter.*

> "Taco is very proud of his quarter. He doesn't know what it is, but he knows it's his." — Callum Delacroix

## Finding the fifth grade

The town took the request seriously. The retired schoolteacher, who had taught that very class in 1976, pulled out her old roll book. Volunteers spent the summer tracking down all fifty names. Some still lived in town. Some had moved to Denver, Seattle, Florida, Germany. Four had died, and their families were invited in their place.

On the last Saturday in September, forty-one of the fifty former fifth-graders, now around sixty years old, came home to Sagebrush Flats for a picnic on the lawn of the old schoolhouse.

They played the cassette tape on a borrowed boombox. It was the class singing, badly, and one boy shouting "HELLO FUTURE!" at the end. Everyone on the lawn shouted it back.

## A new box

Before the day ended, the town buried a new box in Taco's hole. It contains a photograph of the picnic, letters from the forty-one, and a drawing of a border collie by the youngest child in town.

The lid says *DO NOT OPEN UNTIL 2076.*

"And there's a P.S. again," Callum said. "Whoever finds it gets to keep a quarter. Taco donated it."
