Nobody at Larkspur Community Music School wanted the piano. It arrived in March on a flatbed truck, an upright from the 1940s with a cracked walnut case, three dead keys and a smell of old cigars. The estate that donated it had paid for the delivery and nothing else.

The school's resident cat wanted it very much.

Mortimer, a grey tabby adopted from the county shelter two winters ago to keep mice out of the instrument room, climbed on top of the piano the hour it was wheeled in. He stayed there for most of the next three weeks, lying with one paw dangling into the gap between the lid and the case, batting at something nobody else could see.

"We thought he'd found a mouse," said Desmond Achterberg, the school's night custodian for eleven years. "I took the whole top off one night to get it out before it died in there and stank up the building."

There was no mouse. Wedged into the felt behind the hammers, folded twice and yellowed the color of weak tea, was a state lottery ticket dated June 12, 1987.

## Checking the numbers

Desmond almost threw it away. He set it on his cart, finished mopping the recital hall, and only looked at it again at two in the morning because Mortimer would not stop sitting on it.

Out of curiosity he looked up the old drawing on his phone. Then he looked it up again. Then he sat down on the floor of the instrument room.

The ticket matched all six numbers.

"I'm not going to lie to you," Desmond said. "For about ten minutes I thought about everything. My truck needs a transmission. My daughter starts college next year. Ten minutes. Then I thought, this was somebody's. Somebody bought this and folded it up and put it somewhere safe, and it wasn't me."

The state confirmed the ticket was genuine and, under a rule that lets claims be reopened when a prize is found with an estate, still payable to the rightful owner's heirs. The prize, with decades of interest the state holds on unclaimed winnings, came to just over $212,000.

## Four months of phone calls

The piano had come from the estate of a woman named Florence Abiodun, a church organist who died at 96. The estate's lawyer had no record of a ticket and no idea who had played the piano before her.

So Desmond started calling. He called the piano tuner whose sticker was inside the case. He called the moving company that had serviced the house. He spent his lunch breaks in the county records office and his evenings on the phone with strangers.

What he found was this: in 1987 the piano did not belong to Florence. It belonged to her younger brother, Samuel, who ran a bakery on the east side of town and played ragtime for customers on Saturday mornings. Samuel had died in 1988. The bakery was sold. The piano went to his sister.

Samuel's granddaughter, Tamsin Abiodun-Reyes, still bakes. She runs a small storefront called Sam's on the same east-side block. In July she had put a sign in the window: *Closing end of the year. Thank you for everything.*

> "He walked in, a man I'd never met, with a cat in a carrier and a folder of paper, and asked if I was Samuel's granddaughter. I thought I was being sued." — Tamsin Abiodun-Reyes

## The split

Under state rules the prize belonged entirely to Samuel's heirs. Tamsin is the only one.

She insisted on splitting it.

"He could have kept quiet and nobody would ever have known," she said. "My grandfather would have hated that I even thought about it. Half was the only number that felt right."

Desmond argued for a week and lost. His truck has a new transmission. His daughter's first year of tuition is paid.

Sam's is not closing. The sign in the window has been replaced with a framed copy of the 1987 ticket and a photograph of a grey tabby lying on top of an old upright piano, which Tamsin bought back from the music school for one dollar and moved into the corner of the bakery.

On Saturday mornings a teenager from the music school plays ragtime on it. The three dead keys have been fixed.

Mortimer, the school reports, has found a new spot behind the radiator and is pawing at that, too. Desmond says he is not taking that one apart.
