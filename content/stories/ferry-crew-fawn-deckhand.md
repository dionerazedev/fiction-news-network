The 7:15 from Pellham Landing to Cedar Isle is the kind of ferry where everybody knows everybody. The same twelve cars, the same two school kids, the same retired couple with the thermos. So when Captain Dolores Pike spotted a small brown head in the water halfway across the bay one June morning, the whole boat went to the rail.

It was a fawn, maybe three weeks old, swimming in slow, tired circles nearly a mile from either shore.

"Deer swim," Captain Pike said. "Adults swim between the islands all the time. But this one was way too little and way too far out. She was going under."

Deckhand Abel Kittredge leaned over the side with a boat hook and a cargo net. On the third try he got the net under her. The fawn came over the rail, coughed up what seemed like half the bay and collapsed on a pile of life jackets.

## The passenger

The crew dried her with beach towels donated by the school kids and called island wildlife rescue. The plan was simple: dock at Cedar Isle, hand the fawn to the rescue volunteers, and let them find her mother.

The fawn did not agree to the plan.

When the ramp went down she ran to the back of the car deck and wedged herself between a pickup truck and the railing. When volunteers came aboard, she bolted to the bow. When they finally carried her off and set her down in the meadow by the terminal, she stood there for a minute, then walked straight back down the ramp and lay down on the life jackets.

"She had decided the boat was safe," said rescue volunteer Coretta Ames. "And honestly, from her point of view, it was. The boat was the thing that got her out of the water."

## Eleven days on the 7:15

The crew named her Deckhand. For the next eleven days she rode the ferry back and forth: six crossings a day, sleeping on the life jackets, drinking goat's milk formula from a bottle the volunteers brought aboard each morning.

Regular passengers started bringing apple slices. The school kids made her a sign. The retired couple with the thermos, Gus and Henrietta Lorne, gave up their usual seats on the upper deck to sit with her on the car deck every trip.

> "Best eleven days of commuting in my life. I'm not ashamed to say it." — Gus Lorne

Meanwhile, volunteers on Cedar Isle were searching for her mother. Trail cameras on the north end of the island picked up a doe with a torn ear calling at the shoreline every evening, right about where a fawn might have slipped off the rocks.

## The last crossing

On the twelfth morning, Captain Pike tried something new. Instead of docking at the terminal, she brought the ferry in slowly along the north shore, as close as the water allowed, and cut the engines.

The doe with the torn ear was standing on the beach.

Deckhand lifted her head. Her ears went straight up. She walked to the front of the car deck, stood there for a long moment, then jumped into the shallows and splashed the last forty feet to shore.

Every passenger on the boat was at the rail. Nobody said anything until the two deer walked into the trees together.

"Then everyone clapped," Captain Pike said. "On a ferry. At seven in the morning. I'd never heard anything like it."

The life jackets she slept on are still in a pile at the back of the car deck. The crew has not moved them. Every so often, a passenger leaves an apple slice on top.
