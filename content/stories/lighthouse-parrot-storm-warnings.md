The first time Captain Biscuit yelled "Boats in! Boats in!" from the gallery of the Gannet Cove lighthouse, the sky over the harbor was a clean, cloudless blue, and the men on the dock laughed so hard one of them dropped a crate of lobster bands into the water.

Forty minutes later the wind swung hard from the northeast, the temperature fell nine degrees, and a squall line nobody had forecast came across the bay like a wall of slate.

"I had two boats still out past the ledges," said harbormaster Ines Calloway, who has worked the cove for twenty-two years. "They made it back with about five minutes to spare. I remember standing on the dock, soaked, looking up at that bird and thinking, no. That's a coincidence. That has to be a coincidence."

It was not a coincidence. It was, by the town's own careful count, the first of thirty-one.

## A retirement gift nobody wanted

Captain Biscuit is a nineteen-year-old African grey who came to the lighthouse in a cardboard pet carrier the same week as his owner, retired keeper Walter Osei. Walter had spent four decades on oil tankers and wanted, in his words, "a quiet job with a good view and one roommate who doesn't snore."

The parrot had other plans. He learned the ring of the old brass fog bell within a week and the marine radio's weather tone within a month. By winter he could whistle the first four notes of the harbor's noon horn so precisely that the café across the road twice closed for lunch early.

"He's a mimic," Walter said. "That's what greys do. I didn't think much of the 'Boats in' thing. That's what I used to say into the radio on bad nights. He just picked it up."

What Walter didn't notice at first was *when* the bird said it.

## The notebook

It was Ines Calloway's nine-year-old daughter, Pearl, who started writing it down. She did her homework at the harbor office after school and had a habit of keeping lists: gull counts, boat names, the color of the water.

In a green spiral notebook, under the heading BISKIT SAYS, she wrote the date and time every time the parrot shouted his warning. In a second column she wrote down what the weather did next.

By the end of the first summer the second column read, eleven times in a row: *storm.*

> "He was never wrong. Not once. Some days he was early by an hour, some days by twenty minutes, but he was never wrong." — Pearl Calloway, age 9

Pearl brought the notebook to a town meeting in October, stood on a folding chair so she could see over the podium, and read every entry out loud. The room was quiet for a long moment. Then the oldest lobsterman in the cove, a man who had laughed loudest on that first blue afternoon, raised his hand and asked if he could borrow the notebook to copy it.

## What the bird might be hearing

Nobody in Gannet Cove can say for certain how Captain Biscuit does it. Walter's theory is pressure. Birds are sensitive to falling air pressure, he said, and the gallery of a lighthouse sits higher and more exposed than anywhere else in town.

Pearl's theory is that the bird can hear the storm coming across the water "like when you hear a train before you see it."

The bird, asked directly, said "Hello, Walter," and then made the sound of a kettle.

What matters to the cove is the result. Fishing crews now keep one ear on the lighthouse when they leave in the morning. A volunteer sits with a handheld radio on the busiest days and relays the shout out to the boats. In three years, the harbor has not had a single crew caught out past the ledges in a sudden blow.

## The Biscuit Station

This fall the town voted, unanimously, to name its new automated weather station after the bird. A small brass plaque on the mast reads *Captain Biscuit Station: Listen to the Lighthouse.*

At the dedication, Pearl Calloway cut the ribbon. Walter Osei held the parrot on his shoulder, wrapped in a dish towel against the wind. The sky was a clean, cloudless blue.

The crowd had just started to clap when the bird leaned forward, fluffed every feather on his neck, and said, very clearly, "Boats in."

Everyone went home early. It rained for two days.
