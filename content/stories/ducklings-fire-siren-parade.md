The call came in at 6:40 on a May morning: "Strange chirping coming out of the storm drain on Elm and Third."

Engine 3 of the Fenwick Volunteer Fire Department rolled up with lights on and siren wailing, mostly because the driver, a retired dentist named Lou Ferraro, had been waiting all week for an excuse to use it.

Under the grate, fourteen mallard ducklings were paddling in circles in four inches of runoff. Their mother was pacing on the curb, quacking at anyone who came near.

It took the crew forty minutes, a ladder, a fishing net and a colander from the diner across the street to get them all out. Lieutenant Hana Whitcombe lifted the last duckling out of the drain and set it on the sidewalk next to its mother.

"And then Lou, God bless him, hit the siren to celebrate," she said.

## The imprint

Fourteen ducklings and one very startled mother duck took off down Elm Street. But the next morning, when Engine 3 left the station for a routine inspection and sounded its siren at the corner, something odd happened.

A line of fourteen ducklings came marching out of the bushes by the pond and followed the truck.

"We figured it was a fluke," Hana said. "Then it happened the next day. And the next."

Biologists explain that ducklings bond strongly to sights and sounds in their first days. For these fourteen, one of the first loud, comforting sounds after the most frightening morning of their lives was the wail of Engine 3.

> "They don't follow the ambulance. They don't follow the police. They don't even follow Engine 1. Only Engine 3, and only when Lou's driving." — Lt. Hana Whitcombe

## A town that stops for ducks

By midsummer the ducks were half-grown and the "duck parade" had become a Fenwick tradition. Every Saturday at ten, Engine 3 drives slowly from the station to the pond with its siren on a low whoop. The ducks, now full-sized, waddle behind it in a line down the middle of Main Street.

People bring lawn chairs. The diner sells "Duck Parade" pancakes. The police put out traffic cones.

The department says it has been careful. The parade is short, slow and on a closed street, and on emergency calls, crews use a different siren tone the ducks ignore.

## One year later

This May, on the anniversary of the rescue, eleven of the original fourteen ducks were still living at the pond. Several had ducklings of their own.

At the Saturday parade, Lou sounded the siren at the corner as usual. Out of the bushes came the eleven adult ducks. Behind them came twenty-three tiny ducklings, in a wobbling line, following their parents following the fire truck.

Lou had to stop the engine and wipe his eyes.

"I was a dentist for thirty-four years," he said. "Nobody ever followed me anywhere."
