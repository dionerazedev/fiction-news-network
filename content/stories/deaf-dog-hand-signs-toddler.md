Nobody wanted Pepper. She sat in a county shelter for seven months, a white-and-brown Australian shepherd with one blue eye, one brown eye and a card on her kennel that said *Deaf. Needs experienced owner.*

Ingrid Thorsby was not an experienced owner. She was a recently retired court stenographer who had never had a dog. But she had spent forty years watching people's hands while they talked, and she figured that might count for something.

"Everybody told me a deaf dog would be impossible to train," Ingrid said. "I thought: she can't hear me, but she can see me. So I'll talk with my hands."

## Sixty signs

Ingrid started with the basics: a flat palm for *sit*, a pointed finger for *down*, a thumbs-up for *good girl.* Pepper learned them in a week.

Then Ingrid kept going. *Walk. Car. Ball. Wait. Bed. Dinner. Gentle. Find it. Who's here? Go say hello.* She borrowed a library book on American Sign Language and adapted signs she found there. Within a year Pepper knew more than sixty.

"She watches my hands the way other dogs watch a treat," Ingrid said. "She's always paying attention. I think she was just waiting for somebody to talk to her."

## The boy next door

Next door lives three-year-old Wesley Amari-Clarke, who started talking later than most kids his age. His parents, Tomas and Celeste, had begun using simple baby signs with him at the suggestion of his pediatrician: *more, milk, all done, help.*

Wesley was not especially interested. Then he saw Pepper.

From his front window he watched Ingrid in the yard every morning making shapes with her hands, and the big spotted dog sitting, lying down, spinning, fetching. One afternoon, Celeste found him at the window with his palm held flat toward the glass. Outside, Pepper was sitting.

> "He looked back at me with this face like, *I did that.* And I thought, oh. Oh, okay. That's how we do this." — Celeste Amari-Clarke

## The morning class

Now, most mornings, Wesley and his mother walk next door for what the street calls "Pepper class." Ingrid makes a sign. Pepper does it. Wesley copies it. Then Wesley tries it on Pepper, and if Pepper does it, Wesley shrieks with joy and Pepper wags so hard she falls over.

He has learned more than thirty signs. His parents say his spoken words have followed quickly: *Pepper* was among the first, followed by *ball*, *sit* and, memorably, *again.*

His speech therapist has started recommending that other families use pets, puppets or picture cards the same way: something the child is excited to talk to.

## The neighborhood learns too

The habit has spread down Mulberry Lane. The mail carrier waves the sign for *hello* at Pepper every morning. Two kids across the street use the sign for *gentle* with their kitten. At this summer's block party, Ingrid taught a ten-minute sign lesson on her lawn to almost thirty neighbors.

"Seven months in a shelter because she couldn't hear," Ingrid said, watching Pepper and Wesley practice *wait* by the mailbox. "Turns out she was the best listener on the street."
